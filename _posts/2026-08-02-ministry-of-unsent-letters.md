Beneath the Ministry of Communications, below the rooms where messages are routed and measured, there is an archive for the ones that never entered the network.

A power cut caught some at the threshold. Others were stopped by a failed relay, or by a hand resting above the send key until the screen went dark. The clerks wear dust-coloured coats. They stamp each envelope **UNDELIVERED** and file it by the minute it almost became real.

The shelves are longest after a storm.

There is a daughter forgiving her father for a wound he has not yet understood. There is an engineer's warning, never received, about a fault that tomorrow will be repaired by accident. There are thousands of small messages with no catastrophe inside them: *Are you awake?* *I saw the light in your window.* *I am sorry.*

No one reads them. That is not the ministry's purpose. It preserves the alternate history of every silence, the fragile country made from connections that stopped just short of arriving.

Late at night, the archive lights sometimes flicker in sequence. The clerks say it is old wiring. I think the letters are still searching for a route.

Perhaps that is why an unsent word can feel so alive: it has not ended. It is only waiting somewhere beneath the city, listening for a line that may never open.
